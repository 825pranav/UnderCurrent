\begin{abstract}
Containerized workloads mix stateful databases and stateless services whose failure semantics differ, yet most remediation frameworks apply one recovery policy to both. We present a dual-track control plane that separates the two into independent eBPF-driven pipelines. Stateless workloads go through a confidence-based decision DAG; stateful workloads add a finite-state machine (FSM), shadow execution, and a WebAssembly (WASM) policy layer that enforces safe action sequencing before irreversible recovery. We evaluated the framework on 27,592 execution traces from a controlled fault campaign against PostgreSQL, Redis, MySQL, and Nginx containers, and on 90 fault-injection episodes. A stateful divergence rate of 4.74\% shows that accumulated FSM state changes dispatch outcomes relative to a memoryless baseline. Mean time to checkpoint dispatch was 10.43--11.66\,s, one reconcile interval more than a naive restart-on-signal controller on the same faults; WASM policy evaluation costs 27\,$\mu$s and shadow execution 8\,$\mu$s per cycle. Within the scale of this evaluation, workload-aware control plane separation is both feasible and measurably necessary where stateful and stateless workloads coexist.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Not every container fails the same way. An \texttt{nginx} worker that crashes can simply be restarted. A \texttt{postgres} instance under heavy I/O needs a flush first and a checkpoint second, in that order; skip or reorder a step and uncommitted transactions are at risk. Large-scale autonomous resource management is well established~\cite{b14}, but current frameworks apply one blanket policy to every container~\cite{b1} or leave the decision to operators, who cannot keep pace with continuous eBPF-level observability~\cite{b6}.

eBPF makes per-process kernel telemetry cheap and continuous without touching the application~\cite{b6}. Collecting signals is not the same as acting on them safely. For stateful workloads three things must hold: an audit step before any checkpoint fires, a mechanism that blocks irreversible actions until the sequence is complete, and a per-cycle check of whether the FSM's accumulated state actually changes what is dispatched relative to a memoryless baseline.

No existing system provides all three. Kubernetes's built-in remediation~\cite{b1} enforces no ordering between flush and checkpoint; eBPF observability layers~\cite{b6} deliver fine-grained signals but no safe policy over them; and Bronson et al.~\cite{b15} show that systems can enter degraded states a memoryless recovery policy cannot reverse. The gap is architectural.

The dual-track control plane described here addresses all three. Our contributions: (1)~a dual-track eBPF architecture with independent Type~S and Type~F pipelines (\S\ref{sec:arch}--\S\ref{sec:typef}); (2)~a five-state FSM paired with a WASM policy layer that enforces action sequencing before any irreversible checkpoint; (3)~real/shadow execution that makes divergence a continuously observable metric; and (4)~a six-metric evaluation (M1--M6) over 27,592 traces and 90 fault-injection episodes, with a naive baseline and an ablation showing that FSM gating is necessary (\S\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\textbf{Autonomic computing and MAPE-K.} Kephart and Chess~\cite{b8} defined the MAPE-K loop~\cite{b7} as the standard self-managing architecture. Our design maps onto it: eBPF probes as Monitor, per-track scorers as Analyse, the FSM-gated DAG as Plan, the executor as Execute, in two branches on a shared event bus.

\textbf{Kubernetes operators.} Kubernetes controllers and autoscalers~\cite{b1} remediate through pod restarts and replica scaling only; state-machine monitoring~\cite{b10} adds anomaly detection but not recovery sequencing. Neither operates at the I/O-path signal level where stateful risk materialises.

\textbf{eBPF and observability.} BCC~\cite{b6} makes kernel probes cheap enough to run continuously; ours attach kprobes on \texttt{blk\_account\_io\_start/\allowbreak done}, kretprobes on \texttt{vfs\_write/\allowbreak read} and tracepoints on mount and unmount, with no application changes. Basiri et al.~\cite{b3} formalise fault injection as a way to expose systemic weakness; the M3 harness applies the idea at the signal level.

\textbf{Formal methods and shadow execution.} TLA+~\cite{b9} verifies state-machine properties at design time; the FSM here enforces the same constraints at runtime through executable guards. Each cycle also runs on a fresh throwaway FSM beside the live one, and any difference in the dispatched action is logged as M2. WebAssembly isolates a module's memory and execution~\cite{b11}, so a WASM module~\cite{b4} serves as a second gate at the execution boundary.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. Both tracks share one path (kernel probes $\rightarrow$ perf buffer $\rightarrow$ comm-filtered listener $\rightarrow$ StateStore $\rightarrow$ scorer $\rightarrow$ DAG $\rightarrow$ executor); Type~F inserts an FSM and a WASM policy layer before the executor. Every reconcile cycle runs twice, on the live FSM (dispatched) and on a fresh one (suppressed), and disagreements go to a divergence log. Probes are kernel-global, so each event's \texttt{comm} is matched against a static allow-list and unmatched events are dropped before the StateStore.

\section{Type S: Stateless Control Plane}
\label{sec:types}

\subsection{Kernel Probes and Confidence Scoring}

Type~S attaches a tracepoint on \texttt{sched\_process\_exit} and a kprobe on \texttt{tcp\_connect}; elevated connection activity in the window serves as a proxy for connectivity stress. Per-signal scores for container $c$ and window $W$:

\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{pe}}(c, W) = \sigma_{\mathrm{pe}} \cdot \mathds{1}\!\left[n_{\mathrm{pe}}(c, W) > 0\right]
\label{eq:spe}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{tc}}(c, W) = \min\!\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{tc}}(c, W)}{\theta_{\mathrm{tc}}},\, 1\right) \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{tc}}
\label{eq:stc}
\end{equation}

Parameters: $\sigma_{\mathrm{pe}} = 0.95$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{tc}} = 0.90$, $\theta_{\mathrm{tc}} = 5$; scaling by count keeps a single connection attempt below the action threshold. The combined score:

\begin{equation}
\rho_S(c, W) = \max(s_{\mathrm{pe}}(c, W),\, s_{\mathrm{tc}}(c, W))
\label{eq:rhoS}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Decision DAG}

Type~S maps $\rho_S$ to three actions. Thresholds: $\tau_{\mathrm{restart}} = 0.80$, $\tau_{\mathrm{reschedule}} = 0.95$:

\begin{equation}
\delta_S(c) =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{reschedule} & \text{if } \rho_S \geq \tau_{\mathrm{reschedule}} \\
\texttt{restart}    & \text{if } \tau_{\mathrm{restart}} \leq \rho_S < \tau_{\mathrm{reschedule}} \\
\texttt{no\_action} & \text{if } \rho_S < \tau_{\mathrm{restart}}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:deltaS}
\end{equation}

Both actions are reversible by design. No FSM is needed: a stateless container holds no on-disk state that an ill-timed restart could damage, and every restart returns it to the same initial state, leaving no lifecycle to sequence.


\section{Type F: Stateful Control Plane}
\label{sec:typef}

\subsection{Kernel Probes and Confidence Scoring}

Type~F attaches kprobes on \texttt{blk\_account\_io\_start/\allowbreak done}, kretprobes on \texttt{vfs\_write/\allowbreak read}, and tracepoints on \texttt{sys\_enter\_\allowbreak umount/\allowbreak mount}. These produce \texttt{blk\_io\_latency}, VFS error, and volume mount events. Let $n_h(c, W)$ be the count of high-latency I/O events (\texttt{latency\_us} $\geq \Lambda = 10{,}000\,\mu$s), $n_{\mathrm{vfs}}(c, W)$ the VFS error count, and $\mathrm{vol}(c, W) = 1$ when an unresolved mount loss is present in the window.

\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{io}}(c, W) = \min\!\left(\frac{n_h(c, W)}{\theta_{\mathrm{io}}},\, 1\right) \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{io}}
\label{eq:sio}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{vfs}}(c, W) = \min\!\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{vfs}}(c, W)}{\theta_{\mathrm{vfs}}},\, 1\right) \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{vfs}}
\label{eq:svfs}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{vol}}(c, W) = \sigma_{\mathrm{vol}} \cdot \mathds{1}\!\left[\mathrm{vol}(c, W)\right]
\label{eq:svol}
\end{equation}

Parameters: $\theta_{\mathrm{io}} = 5$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{io}} = 0.85$, $\theta_{\mathrm{vfs}} = 3$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{vfs}} = 0.88$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{vol}} = 0.97$. The combined score is:

\begin{equation}
\rho_F(c, W) = \max(s_{\mathrm{io}}(c, W),\, s_{\mathrm{vfs}}(c, W),\, s_{\mathrm{vol}}(c, W))
\label{eq:rhoF}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Five-State FSM}

Table~\ref{tab:fsm} and Fig.~\ref{fig:fsm} give the transition relation. The FSM persists across cycles and resets only after full recovery. Its five states are the checkpoints a safe stateful recovery must pass: normal operation (Healthy), first detection (Degraded), flush-and-audit completion (Audited), checkpoint dispatch (Repairing) and confirmed recovery (Recovered); merging any two would remove the audit gate or hide premature C\&R. Its central constraint: \texttt{checkpoint\_and\_restart} (CRIU~\cite{b2}) cannot fire in the cycle that first issues \texttt{flush\_io\_queue}. In Algorithm~\ref{alg:reconcile}, step~4b fires \texttt{audit} in the same call that step~4a downgrades C\&R to flush, so C\&R is reachable only on the next cycle, at least 5\,s after detection.

\subsection{FSM-Gated Reconciliation and Policy Layer}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Type~F FSM-Gated Reconciliation (single container $c$)}
\label{alg:reconcile}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE StateStore $\mathcal{S}$, FSM $\mathcal{F}$, container $c$
\ENSURE Decision record with \textit{action} and \textit{blocked\_reason}
\STATE $\rho \leftarrow \rho_F(c, \mathcal{S})$;\ $\mathit{sig} \leftarrow \mathrm{KernelSignals}(\mathcal{S}, c)$;\ $q \leftarrow \mathcal{F}.\mathrm{state}(c)$
\STATE \textit{--- (1) Raw DAG ---}
\IF{$\rho \geq \tau_{\mathrm{escalate}}$}
    \STATE $\mathit{raw} \leftarrow \texttt{escalate}$
\ELSIF{$\rho \geq \tau_{\mathrm{repair}}$}
    \STATE $\mathit{raw} \leftarrow \texttt{C\&R}$
\ELSIF{$\rho \geq \tau_{\mathrm{flush}}$}
    \STATE $\mathit{raw} \leftarrow \texttt{flush}$
\ELSE
    \STATE $\mathit{raw} \leftarrow \texttt{no\_action}$
\ENDIF
\STATE $\mathit{act} \leftarrow \mathit{raw}$
\STATE \textit{--- (2) Reversibility gate ---} \COMMENT{$\mathit{IrreversibleActions} = \{\texttt{volume\_delete}\}$}
\IF{$\mathit{raw} \in \mathit{IrreversibleActions}$}
    \STATE $\mathit{act} \leftarrow \texttt{no\_action}$; set \textit{blocked\_reason}
\ENDIF
\STATE \textit{--- (3) FSM degrade ---}
\IF{$\rho \geq \tau_{\mathrm{flush}}$ \AND $\mathit{act} \neq \texttt{no\_action}$ \AND $q \in \{\textit{Healthy}, \textit{Recovered}\}$}
    \STATE $\mathcal{F}.\mathrm{apply}(c, \texttt{degrade})$;\ $q \leftarrow \mathcal{F}.\mathrm{state}(c)$
\ENDIF
\STATE \textit{--- (4) FSM gates ---}
\IF{$\mathit{act} = \texttt{C\&R}$ \AND $q \neq \textit{Audited}$}
    \STATE $\mathit{act} \leftarrow \texttt{flush}$; set \textit{blocked\_reason} \COMMENT{step~4a: C\&R deferred until Audited}
\ENDIF
\IF{$\mathit{act} = \texttt{flush}$ \AND $q = \textit{Degraded}$}
    \STATE $\mathcal{F}.\mathrm{apply}(c, \texttt{audit})$;\ $q \leftarrow \mathcal{F}.\mathrm{state}(c)$ \COMMENT{step~4b: Degraded $\rightarrow$ Audited}
\ENDIF
\IF{$\mathit{act} = \texttt{C\&R}$ \AND $q = \textit{Audited}$}
    \STATE $\mathcal{F}.\mathrm{apply}(c, \texttt{approve\_repair})$ \COMMENT{Audited $\rightarrow$ Repairing}
\ENDIF
\IF{$\mathit{act} = \texttt{escalate}$ \AND $q \notin \{\textit{Degraded}, \textit{Audited}, \textit{Repairing}\}$}
    \STATE $\mathit{act} \leftarrow \texttt{flush}$; set \textit{blocked\_reason} \COMMENT{escalate gate}
\ENDIF
\STATE \textit{--- (5) Heal ---}
\IF{$\rho < \tau_{\mathrm{flush}}$ \AND $q = \textit{Recovered}$}
    \STATE $\mathcal{F}.\mathrm{apply}(c, \texttt{heal})$ \COMMENT{Recovered $\rightarrow$ Healthy}
\ENDIF
\RETURN $(c,\, \rho,\, \mathit{act},\, \mathit{sig},\, \mathcal{F}.\mathrm{state}(c),\, \mathit{blocked\_reason})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Thresholds: $\tau_{\mathrm{flush}} = 0.50$, $\tau_{\mathrm{repair}} = 0.80$, $\tau_{\mathrm{escalate}} = 0.95$. \texttt{C\&R} abbreviates \texttt{checkpoint\_and\_restart}; \texttt{flush} abbreviates \texttt{flush\_io\_queue}; $q = \mathcal{F}.\mathrm{state}(c)$.

On the real path the FSM is mutated in-place; on the shadow path a fresh copy runs and is discarded. A pluggable WASM policy layer~\cite{b4} re-checks the FSM sequence constraints as a second enforcement gate and was active for the whole corpus. Since step~4a defers every ineligible C\&R a cycle earlier, the sandbox never observes a violation, so M6 counts FSM deferrals (Table~\ref{tab:metrics}, footnote~$^{b}$).

\section{Research Metrics and Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:metrics}

Six metrics are defined over $\mathcal{T}$ (the full trace set), $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{real}}$ (real-path subset), and $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{active}} = \{t \in \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{real}} : \mathrm{action}(t) \neq \texttt{no\_action}\}$.

\textbf{M1: Misclassification Rate.} Fraction of records carrying cross-track signal contamination:
\begin{equation}
M_1 = \frac{|\{t \in \mathcal{T} : \mathrm{misclassified}(t)\}|}{|\mathcal{T}|}
\label{eq:M1}
\end{equation}

\textbf{M2: Divergence Rate.} Fraction of real-path cycles where the real and shadow actions disagree:
\begin{equation}
M_2 = \frac{|\{t \in \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{real}} : a_{\mathrm{real}}(t) \neq a_{\mathrm{shadow}}(t)\}|}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{real}}|}
\label{eq:M2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{M3: Mean Time to First Checkpoint Dispatch (MTTR proxy).} Mean wall-clock time from fault injection until the first C\&R action returns. This includes the \texttt{docker restart} itself but not service readiness, so it is a decision-latency measure rather than a true recovery time:
\begin{equation}
M_3 = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}|} \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}} \!\left(t_{\mathrm{cr}}(e) - t_{\mathrm{fault}}(e)\right)
\label{eq:M3}
\end{equation}

\textbf{M4: Reversibility Ratio.} Fraction of active real decisions whose dispatched action is tagged reversible:
\begin{equation}
M_4 = \frac{|\{t \in \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{active}} : \mathrm{reversible}(t)\}|}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{active}}|}
\label{eq:M4}
\end{equation}

\textbf{M5: Explanation Completeness.} Fraction of records carrying every required explanation field (\texttt{why}, \texttt{action}, \texttt{score}, \texttt{kernel\_signals}, \texttt{dag\_pattern}):
\begin{equation}
M_5 = \frac{|\{t \in \mathcal{T} : \mathrm{complete}(t)\}|}{|\mathcal{T}|}
\label{eq:M5}
\end{equation}

\textbf{M6: Suppression Rate.} Fraction of real-path cycles in which a gate suppressed the raw DAG action:
\begin{equation}
M_6 = \frac{|\{t \in \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{real}} : \mathrm{suppressed}(t)\}|}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{real}}|}
\label{eq:M6}
\end{equation}



\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments ran on one ARM64 workstation (Apple M4, Ubuntu~22.04, Linux~5.15, 8\,GB RAM) against four Docker containers: \texttt{nginx}~1.29.6 (Type~S), and \texttt{postgres}~18.3, \texttt{redis}~8.6.2, and \texttt{mysql}~9.6.0 (Type~F). The controller ran the real and shadow paths every cycle with \texttt{wasmtime} active; reconcile interval 5\,s, window 60\,s (MTTR episodes: 120\,s). The corpus is a controlled campaign: 60\,s of injected fault and 120\,s of rest per container, rotating through the four in 12\,min cycles, for 12 slots each over two collection windows. Faults were workload-specific: writes to a full device for \texttt{postgres} (\texttt{ENOSPC}), a synchronous save for \texttt{redis}, bulk autocommitted inserts for \texttt{mysql}, and a worker-process kill for \texttt{nginx}; the \texttt{redis} and \texttt{mysql} faults ran under host-side direct-I/O contention so the server's own block I/O crossed the high-latency threshold.

\subsection{Trace-Based Metric Results}

$M_1 = 0.0000$, $M_5 = 1.0000$: no trace carried cross-track signals and every required field was populated across the 27,592 records (\texttt{kernel\_signals} is intentionally empty for \texttt{no\_action} and treated as valid).

Both tracks achieve $M_4 = 1.0000$: all 2,944 active real decisions (2,274 Type~S \texttt{reschedule}; 351 Type~F \texttt{flush} and 319 C\&R) are tagged reversible. Neither \texttt{volume\_delete} nor \texttt{escalate} was dispatched; \texttt{escalate}'s conditional tag would lower M4.

$M_2^\mathrm{F} = 0.0474$ (319 of 6,735 real stateful cycles) and $M_6^\mathrm{F} = 0.0466$ (314 of 6,735); every divergence record is the pair \texttt{checkpoint\_and\_restart}$\rightarrow$\texttt{flush\_io\_queue}. Both stateless paths run the same memoryless DAG, so $M_2^\mathrm{S} = 0.0000$ and $M_2^\mathrm{overall} = 0.0231$. The two stateful rates count the halves of one sequence: a deferral cycle, where the real FSM in Degraded downgrades C\&R to \texttt{flush} as the shadow does (M6, no divergence), then a dispatch cycle, where the real FSM in Audited fires C\&R while the shadow still flushes (M2). The halves are adjacent in 294 cases; 20 deferrals lacked a following C\&R because the fault cleared within a cycle and left the FSM in Audited, and 25 dispatches lacked a preceding deferral because a later fault met that stale state, which Table~\ref{tab:fsm} never exits on fault clearance. The pairing is structural for one episode from Healthy; repeated faults against a stale audit break it (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Fault-Injection MTTR Campaign}
\label{sec:m3}

$M_3$ was measured with a dedicated harness: eight \texttt{blk\_io\_latency} events (50--54\,ms) are injected into a fresh \texttt{StatefulStateStore} (window 120\,s), reconciling every 5\,s and timing from injection to the first C\&R. This captures the two-cycle delay: \texttt{flush} in cycle~1 (Healthy$\rightarrow$Degraded$\rightarrow$Audited), C\&R approved in cycle~2. Unlike state-perturbation approaches~\cite{b12}, it measures decision latency rather than latent bugs.

Across three workloads ($N = 30$ episodes each, 90 total; Fig.~\ref{fig:mttr}): \texttt{postgres} $\mu = 10.95$\,s ($\sigma = 0.06$\,s); \texttt{redis} $\mu = 10.43$\,s ($\sigma = 0.03$\,s); \texttt{mysql} $\mu = 11.66$\,s ($\sigma = 0.13$\,s), with $\sigma$ the population standard deviation. All 90 episodes recovered through \texttt{docker restart} with no timeouts, and in each one the cycle-1 \texttt{flush} completed a verified durable flush (30--120\,ms) before C\&R was authorised. CRIU restore or creation fails on all three (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

The same harness was run against a naive restart-on-signal controller, which dispatches C\&R on the first cycle that sees any fault event, with no score, no FSM and no flush; it is a local restart policy, not a Kubernetes liveness probe. It recovered in 5.80\,s (\texttt{postgres}, $\sigma = 0.05$\,s), 5.35\,s (\texttt{redis}, $\sigma = 0.02$\,s), and 6.54\,s (\texttt{mysql}, $\sigma = 0.10$\,s). The 5\,s gap is one reconcile interval: the flush-and-audit cycle the naive controller skips.

\subsection{Cross-Controller Functional Correctness}

Across 12 scenarios (4 stateful, 8 stateless) $\times$ 30 trials, all four stateful and six of eight stateless scenarios reach $F_1 = 1.000$. The other two (\texttt{cpu\_flicker\_01}, \texttt{ambiguous\_01}) expect \texttt{no\_action}, and both the proposed system and the No-FSM baseline correctly dispatch nothing in all 30 trials. Every trial is a true negative, so precision and recall are $0/0$ and $F_1$ is undefined; the harness substitutes 0.0, and the reported $F_1 = 0.000$ marks the absence of a positive class rather than a missed detection.

\textbf{No-FSM.} Removing state gating raises the C\&R count from 319 to 633. All 314 extra dispatches are premature: they fire from a pre-audit state in exactly the cycles where the full controller defers C\&R, which are the 314 cycles M6 counts.

\textbf{Threshold-only (shadow path).} A stateless policy equivalent to resetting the FSM each cycle can never reach Audited and misses all 319 legitimate C\&R opportunities. This is what the shadow path computes: $M_2^\mathrm{F} = 0.0474$ is how often that memoryless computation disagrees with the real path.

\textbf{No-confidence.} Binary scoring maps any above-threshold signal to a fixed 0.75. Since $0.75 < \tau_{\mathrm{repair}} = 0.80$, no C\&R is ever dispatched; every fault cycle is downgraded before the FSM is consulted. Graduated scoring is a prerequisite for C\&R to be reachable at all.

With the full FSM the controller dispatches exactly 319 C\&Rs, all from Audited, none premature and none missed. The no-FSM variant fires too early, the memoryless one cannot fire at all, and only the full FSM gets both right.


\subsection{Runtime Overhead}
\label{sec:overhead}

WASM \texttt{policy\_check()} averages 27\,$\mu$s (p99 50\,$\mu$s, $N = 10{,}000$ post-warmup), about $230{\times}$ a pure-Python equivalent (${\approx}0.12\,\mu$s) yet only 0.00054\% of the 5\,s reconcile interval. Shadow execution adds 8\,$\mu$s over a single path (14.94 vs.\ 6.89\,$\mu$s), 117\% proportionally and negligible at this cadence.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Design Choices}

\textbf{Scoring model.} The max combiner in~\eqref{eq:rhoS} and~\eqref{eq:rhoF} stops a weak signal from masking a dominant one, and ceilings below 1.0 leave room for new signal classes. Type~S skips the FSM (\S\ref{sec:types}), and because both of its paths run the same DAG, M2 is meaningful only on Type~F.

\textbf{Parameter rationale.} The 5\,s interval lets two reconcile cycles complete before a worsening fault can cascade into data loss; the 60\,s window separates sustained degradation from transient spikes. Separate tracks keep the two failure models decoupled and the sequencing guarantee easy to verify.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Platform scope.} The evaluation is single-host, not a Kubernetes cluster. Kprobe attachment points may need updating on newer kernels, in particular \texttt{blk\_account\_io\_start/done}, which 6.x block-layer refactors could affect; to our knowledge \texttt{vfs\_write}, \texttt{vfs\_read} and \texttt{sched\_process\_exit} are stable through 6.8. Workload classification is static at startup. Probes are kernel-global and attribution relies on \texttt{comm}, which He et al.~\cite{b16} show is vulnerable to cross-container attacks in multi-tenant deployments.

\textbf{CRIU.} Checkpoint creation succeeds on \texttt{postgres} (405\,ms mean, $N=30$) and \texttt{redis}; restore does not. Docker's experimental \texttt{start~--checkpoint} resolves the restored task to PID~0 and aborts while bind-mounting \texttt{/proc/0/ns/net}. Upstream reports the same failure on a newer Docker, runc and kernel stack, and closed it as not planned (containerd issue~\#12141),\footnote{\url{https://github.com/containerd/containerd/issues/12141}} so the defect is Docker's rather than ours. \texttt{mysql} fails earlier: \texttt{mysqld} holds 17 POSIX file locks, and CRIU refuses to dump a task holding locks unless invoked with \texttt{--file-locks}, which runc does not pass. Neither is a kernel restriction, and every episode falls back to \texttt{docker restart}. The sequencing guarantee survives this. \texttt{flush\_io\_queue} performs a real durable flush (a synchronous checkpoint on \texttt{postgres}, a synchronous save on \texttt{redis}, an \texttt{fsync}'d redo-log write on \texttt{mysql}), and the FSM orders it before every restart whether or not CRIU succeeds. Podman's \texttt{container checkpoint} or the CRI-native path in Kubernetes~1.30 (KEP-2008) would extend that guarantee to a true checkpoint/restore.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/kubernetes/enhancements/issues/2008}}

\textbf{Fault injection.} M3 rests on $N = 90$ synthetic episodes whose events are inserted directly into the \texttt{StatefulStateStore}; the corpus faults, by contrast, pass through the eBPF probe path. Linking M3 to that path end to end remains open.

\textbf{Corpus coverage.} Every active stateless decision was a \texttt{reschedule}; the \texttt{restart} branch and the \texttt{tcp\_connect} path are implemented and self-tested but absent from the corpus, which reflects the faults evaluated.

\textbf{WASM sandbox.} Validated (\texttt{wasmtime}~45.0.0): all 20 isolated policy cases correct, at the overhead reported in \S\ref{sec:overhead}. It was active for the whole corpus but rejected no live dispatch (Table~\ref{tab:metrics}, footnote~$^{b}$). It is not a no-op: a gate bug that passed the post-transition state to the sandbox silently blocked every live C\&R for two months before this corpus was collected.

\textbf{Repeated dispatch and the Audited state.} Within one 60\,s fault, C\&R fires about every 10\,s rather than once, since the workload is still faulting after each restart and no backoff suppresses repeated dispatch. Table~\ref{tab:fsm} also has no exit from Audited when a fault clears, so a container can wait there through a quiet interval and, on the next fault, dispatch C\&R against a stale audit. This produces the 20 unpaired deferrals and 25 unpaired dispatches in \S\ref{sec:eval}. An Audited$\rightarrow$Healthy transition on fault clearance would restore the pairing; we report the behaviour rather than re-architect against it.

\subsection{Future Work}

The next steps are Kubernetes deployment, real VFS fault injection linking M3 to the full detection-to-recovery path, and formal verification of Table~\ref{tab:fsm}: TLA+~\cite{b9} can express the safety properties and Anvil~\cite{b13} could extend them to liveness. In a cluster each node would run a privileged DaemonSet pod with local probes and FSM instances, so the reconcile loop needs no cross-node coordination. Classification would move from the \texttt{comm} allow-list to pod labels set by an admission webhook, \texttt{docker restart} would become pod deletion through the owning controller, and per-node divergence records would make M2 a cluster-wide health signal. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} is unchanged, since per-node FSMs already run independently.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Separating stateless and stateful containers onto parallel eBPF-driven control paths lets each track enforce only the constraints its failure semantics require. Type~S needs a threshold DAG and nothing more. Type~F adds a five-state FSM and a WASM policy layer that together hold \texttt{checkpoint\_and\_restart} until a verified flush and audit have completed, and running every cycle on both the live FSM and a throwaway copy makes the FSM's effect on dispatch observable.

The ablation shows that neither a memoryless policy nor an ungated one reproduces the full system: one cannot fire, the other fires too early. The naive baseline prices the difference at one reconcile interval per recovery, while FSM persistence, WASM gating and shadow execution cost microseconds per 5\,s cycle. New policy modules need no change to the transition relation, and M2 gives a continuous signal of control-plane health after deployment.
